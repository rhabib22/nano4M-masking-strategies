Masking Strategies for nano4M

How the pre-training mask shapes what a small multimodal model learns

Ricardo Habib, Ralph Chidiac, Nacer Saadani-Hassani, Gabriel Chidiac
École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne
COM-304 Foundation Models · Spring 2026 · Team 11
Report (PDF) BibTeX

Abstract

We study how the choice of masking strategy at pre-training affects what a small multimodal model learns. Building on nano4M, we train five variants on CLEVR with identical architecture, data, compute and seeds, varying only the masking strategy: random (V0), 2D block (V1), context-block (V2), text-side contiguous span (V3), and a per-sample 50/50 mix of V0 and V2 (V4). We evaluate every variant under one unified protocol across reconstruction and cross-modal generation, scoring with seven complementary metrics. Three different masks produce three different specialists. V2 wins the meaning-aware and object-grounded metrics in both generation directions. V1 wins pixel-level reconstruction. V4 wins lexical caption similarity in image-to-caption, a metric neither of its constituent strategies leads alone. V3 ties with the random baseline on every text metric, which we report as an honest negative result attributable to visual grounding being the bottleneck in our four-modality setup.

Key takeaways

  • The mask is the objective. Different pre-training masks produce different specialist behaviours, even with identical architecture, data, compute and seeds.
  • Context-block masking (V2) forces cross-modal binding by hiding most of every image-side modality. It beats the random baseline on parser field accuracy by a factor of 2.4 and tops the LLM judge and the object-detection F1.
  • Block masking (V1) wins pixel reconstruction. Removing the local-neighbour shortcut sharpens depth boundaries and RGB texture.
  • A 50/50 mix of random and context-block (V4) beats both of its parents on lexical caption similarity, a metric neither pure mask leads alone.
  • Text-side span masking (V3) ties with the random baseline on every text metric. We discuss this as a useful negative result that locates the bottleneck, not as a failure.

Introduction

Multimodal masked models like 4M learn to fill in hidden tokens across multiple modalities. The masking strategy at pre-training is the only thing that defines this self-supervised objective. Random masking is the default choice, but it has a subtle weakness. When a token is hidden, its immediate neighbours in the same modality usually stay visible, so the model can solve much of its training loss by predicting from local context. Cross-modal binding (using depth to predict text, or text to predict pixels) stays optional rather than necessary.

This raises an empirical question. If random masking lets the model skip cross-modal binding, does a more constrained mask force the model to learn fundamentally different skills? And do those skills transfer to downstream cross-modal generation?

We test this on nano4M, a small four-modality version of 4M trained on CLEVR. We train five variants under matched conditions: identical FourM backbone (encoder and decoder depth 6, model dimension 512), identical 5-billion-token training budget, identical optimizer, matched random seeds. Only the masking strategy varies between variants. The five strategies cover the obvious axes. V0 is the random baseline. V1 hides contiguous square blocks of image tokens, removing the local-neighbour shortcut. V2 keeps a single small visible block per image modality, hiding around 90 percent of every image-side input. V3 hides contiguous spans of the caption itself, with span lengths calibrated to CLEVR field length. V4 alternates V0 and V2 per training sample.

We evaluate every variant under one unified protocol on the CLEVR validation set. Per-modality reconstruction and cross-modal generation in both directions. Seven metrics covering complementary aspects of behaviour. Three random seeds per variant. Our headline finding is that different masks produce different specialists, and the specialism is predictable from the structure of the mask.

Method

Setup

The base model is nano4M, a four-modality FourM encoder-decoder transformer with encoder depth 6, decoder depth 6, and model dimension 512. The four modalities are tokenised RGB, depth and surface-normal maps via the Cosmos image tokenizer (16 by 16 = 256 tokens per modality), plus a scene caption (scene_desc) tokenised with GPT-2 to up to 256 tokens. Training runs for 5 billion tokens at batch size 256, approximately 38,000 iterations. The optimizer, data, and seeds are all identical across variants. Only the masking strategy varies.

For each training sample, two integers are drawn independently and uniformly from [1, 128]. One is the encoder-input budget, the other is the decoder-target budget. A per-sample Dirichlet then splits each budget across the four modalities. Within each modality, the masking strategy decides which specific positions become encoder input and which become decoder target. Positions selected by neither are unused for that sample. The two integer ranges, the batch size, and the total token budget are identical across all variants and ablations.

The five masking strategies

V0 baseline. Image-side input and target positions are sampled uniformly at random from the 256-position grid. Text-side positions are also random within scene_desc. This is the original 4M masking recipe and the reference all other variants are compared against.

V1 block. For each image modality, random s by s blocks (with s drawn from {2, 3, 4} per sample) are placed on the 16 by 16 grid until enough positions are covered for both budgets. The input and target positions for that modality are then drawn from inside the block region. This removes the local-neighbour shortcut by clustering both input and target into block-shaped regions. Text-side masking stays at V0.

V2 ctxblock. For each image modality, a single s by s visible block (with s drawn from {4, 5, 6} per sample, block position sampled independently per modality) is kept as encoder input. Target tokens are then scattered outside the block. With around 10 percent of every image-side modality visible on average, the caption becomes the only substantially-visible scaffold and the model is forced to rely on cross-modal context. Text-side masking stays at V0.

V3 span. The text modality gets contiguous-span masking. Span lengths are drawn from a geometric distribution with mean 3 and placed at random non-overlapping positions in scene_desc until the target budget is filled. The mean span length was calibrated empirically against CLEVR. The median GPT-2 tokenised length of a "Field: value" phrase in scene_desc is exactly 3 tokens, so a typical span hides one whole semantic field such as "Color: yellow" or "Shape: cube". Image modalities keep V0 random masking.

V4 mixed. Per training sample, a Bernoulli(0.5) coin is flipped. Heads applies V0 masking to that sample, tails applies V2. The model sees both objectives during training. The mixture ratio was set at the TA's suggestion.

Controlled ablations

We further train six controlled ablations to test the sensitivity of V1 and V2 to their block-size hyperparameter. V1 with the block size fixed at 2, 3, and 4 individually (block_b2, block_b3, block_b4). V2 with the ctxblock size fixed at 4, 5, and 6 individually (ctxblock_c4, ctxblock_c5, ctxblock_c6). Each ablation is a full retraining run from scratch with the corresponding size held fixed instead of sampled, isolating the effect of size-sampling itself.

Training dynamics

We trained all five main variants under matched conditions, varying only the masking strategy. Figure 6 confirms this visually: training loss for all five main variants follows the same shape across the full 5-billion-token budget. No variant diverged, oscillated, or stalled. V2 ctxblock converges to a marginally lower value than the other four, which we attribute to its training objective being slightly easier to fit (each sample has a single small visible image-block plus many target tokens, so the model has both a clear scaffold to condition on and a large prediction signal at every step). This is not the same as downstream quality, which the headline evaluation in the next section measures separately.

Experiments and results

Evaluation protocol

We evaluate every variant under one unified protocol on a held-out subset of the CLEVR val split, namely the first 500 sorted-by-filename samples. Each variant is evaluated three times with seeds 0, 1, and 2 (same checkpoint, same 500 samples, only the stochastic token-sampling RNG differs). Per-sample metric values are averaged across seeds.

Two task families. Phase A reconstruction: hide one modality and rebuild it from the other three. Phase B cross-modal generation: give the model a single modality and generate another from scratch, in both directions (image to caption, caption to image). All generation uses an 8-step parallel iterative decoder, matched across variants.

Seven metrics covering complementary aspects of behaviour:

  • Parser field accuracy (sd/pos): regex parser on the generated caption, per-object position fields compared exactly to ground truth.
  • CIDEr-D: TF-IDF weighted n-gram overlap, computed on Phase A scene_desc reconstruction (CIDEr A) and Phase B.1 image-to-caption (CIDEr B).
  • Depth AbsRel and RGB SSIM: pixel-level reconstruction quality on Phase A.
  • LLM judge: Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct scores semantic alignment between generated and ground-truth captions on Phase B.1.
  • Object-detection F1: GroundingDINO-tiny runs on the generated image with the objects parsed from the ground-truth caption as queries, F1 over the resulting set, Phase B.2 only.

Findings

Finding 1: V2 ctxblock wins on meaning-aware and object-grounded metrics in both directions. V2 takes the highest parser field accuracy by a large margin (0.519 against 0.216 baseline, a 2.4x relative improvement at placing objects), the highest CIDEr A on Phase A reconstruction (1.54 against 1.02), the highest LLM judge on Phase B.1 (0.122 against 0.103) and the highest object-detection F1 on Phase B.2 (0.659 against 0.633). The three metrics that actually look at semantic or object-level content all agree V2 is the strongest variant. The mechanism is consistent with the training objective. With around 90 percent of every image-side modality hidden during training, the only substantially-visible scaffold is the caption, so the model is pushed to bind image tokens to text, and that binding pays off at generation time.

Finding 2: V1 block wins pixel-level reconstruction. V1 achieves the lowest depth AbsRel (0.027 against 0.029) and the highest RGB SSIM (0.888 against 0.883). Block masking removes the local-neighbour shortcut by forcing the model to predict from non-adjacent context, which is what sharper depth and RGB textures need.

Finding 3: V4 mixed wins lexical caption similarity in image-to-caption. V4 wins CIDEr B with 0.94, beating both of its parents (V0 = 0.89, V2 = 0.78), and beating V2 in particular, which is the worst on this lexical metric. Image-to-caption needs both broad token coverage (V0's strength) and cross-modal binding (V2's strength). Neither pure mask wins this row alone. The hybrid does.

Finding 4: V3 span ties with V0 baseline. On every text-side metric, V3 sits within one seed standard deviation of V0. Parse rate 0.998 vs 0.999. CIDEr A 1.00 vs 1.02. sd/pos 0.220 vs 0.216. LLM judge 0.097 vs 0.103. CIDEr B 0.82 vs 0.89. The only metric where V3 nudges ahead beyond seed noise is parser/color (0.058 vs 0.048). We report this as a negative result. Text-side span masking, even when calibrated to the data (mean span length 3 matches the median CLEVR Field:value tokenised length), did not meaningfully improve text generation over V0 at our 5-billion-token budget. Our reading is threefold. First, CLEVR's per-field vocabulary is small (three shapes, eight colors, two materials), and V0 already saturates these marginals. Second, text is one of four modalities in the loss, so the masking-strategy gradient is diluted by the three image modalities. Third, the bottleneck is visual grounding (unchanged by V3) rather than text-side prediction. None of this invalidates the V3 design. It locates the bottleneck.

Ablations

The six block-size ablations confirm that V1's pixel-reconstruction win is broadly robust to the block size (depth AbsRel = 0.027 at block_b3 and block_b4, 0.028 at block_b2; RGB SSIM is also stable across the three values). V2's wins, in contrast, depend on size-sampling. Every ctxblock_c4 / c5 / c6 ablation underperforms V2 main on field accuracy, CIDEr A, LLM judge and DINO F1, often substantially (CIDEr B drops from V2 main's 0.78 to 0.32 at ctxblock_c5 and 0.30 at ctxblock_c6). The random sampling across {4, 5, 6}, not any single fixed size, is what makes V2 a robust cross-modal binder. The full table including all six ablations is in the appendix of the report PDF.

Limitations

Our experiments are confined to CLEVR, which has a small per-field vocabulary; rankings may shift on richer language data. Three seeds per variant suffices to confirm the large gaps we report (V2's 2.4x field-accuracy lead) are outside seed variability, but is not enough to claim significance for the smaller gaps. The per-sample LLM-judge / parser agreement on Phase B.1 is low (around 0.10); we therefore treat the LLM judge as a supplementary signal, not the primary one for text generation.

Future work

Two natural directions. First, an alternative V3 implementation using canonical T5-style span corruption under a fine-tuning regime, since T5 was designed as a pre-train-then-fine-tune objective. Second, exploring alternative iterative-decoding orders (centre-outward spiral, anchor-then-fill, raster) instead of the current confidence-first scheme.
